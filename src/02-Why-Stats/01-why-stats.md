## 1. Questions about "Why statistics are important"

### 1.1. Defining key terms

**Question**: Use the space below to define the provided key terms.

- According to the SFSJ authors, what are the main difference between **data** and **information**?: Data is just the raw stuff we collect, but information is what we learn when we analyze and interpret that raw stuff.
- **Variable**: A variable is simply any characteristic that can be different from person to person or situation to situation.
- **Constant**: A constant is a characteristic that is the same for the people or objects that are the focus of the research.
- **Conceptualization**: It is a process that is used in choosing and clearly defining the variable included in the study.
- **Operationalization**: It is like figuring out how to use a measuring tool for something we want to study.
- **Nominal level of data**: It is like organizing things into named categories (with no inherent order)
- **Ordinal level of data**: Organizing data with an inherent order.
- **Interval level of data**: It involves classifying observations that are mutually exclusive, have an inherent order and have equal spacing between categories.
- **Ratio level of data**: It involves classifying observations that are mutually exclusive, have an inherent order, have equal spacing and reflect the absolute magnitude.

### 1.2. On positivism

**Question**: Describe how the S4SJ authors define and use the term **positivism**. How do they suggest it is different from **post-positivism**? According to the authors, why are both paradigms important to social workers? Now, how do you imagine it is important for TPC professionals?

According to the SFSJ authors, positivism treats people like objects that can be measured scientifically, believing "if you can't measure it, it's not real science." Post-positivism says people are complex and we should listen to their voices and experiences, not just measure them. Both are important for social workers because numbers (positivism) help prove problems exist and get funding, while stories (post-positivism) help understand what people actually need. For TPC professionals, this means using data to show what works, and user feedback to understand why things don't work. Together, they create communication that is both effective and truly helpful to real people.

### 1.3. On the "*structural perspective*"

**Question**: Name and define the different parts of the ***structural perspective***. Be sure to cover the parts related practice and research.

The structural perspective provides a comprehensive framework for both practice and research that focuses on changing unfair systems rather than trying to fix individuals who are victims of those systems.

**Answer:** The structural perspective has five parts that work in both helping people and doing research:
1.  Defence of the Client - protect clients from unfair systems and protect research participants from being harmed;  
2. Collectivization - help clients know they're not alone by connecting them to others, and use research to show that many people have the same problems;  
3. Materialization - focus on basic needs like food and housing as the real cause of problems, and study how poverty causes mental health issues; 
4. Increasing Client Power - give clients control by sharing information and letting them make decisions, and let research participants help design and control the research;  
5. Enhancing Client Power Through Personal Change - help clients grow while showing them their problems come from unfair systems, and do research that makes communities stronger

## 2. Questions about "The history of empiricism"

### 2.1. On feminist empiricism

**Question**: According to the SFSJ authors, what is ***feminist empiricism***? And, what issues does it recognize as important to recognize and remedy? Be sure to discuss why it emphasizes the importance of critically reflecting on aspects of the ***context*** of empirical work.

**Answer:** According to the SFSJ authors, feminist empiricism is about fixing science because it was mostly done by men who pretended to be neutral but really ignored women and other groups. The main problems it recognizes are: science excluded women (only 43 women won Nobel Prizes out of 862 total), scientists falsely claimed they had no biases or feelings, and research ignored how the scientist's background affects their work. Feminist empiricism says it's important to think about context because the questions we ask, the theories we use, and the evidence we think is important are all shaped by who is doing the research and what society values. Instead of pretending researchers are robots with no feelings, feminist empiricism says scientists should be honest about their backgrounds, include different people's voices, acknowledge that values and emotions matter, and recognize that science is done by communities, not isolated individuals - basically keeping the good parts of science while making it fair for everyone.

## 3. Questions About "The future of ethics and social justice" in TPC

### 3.1. On the "*socially just ethic*"

**Question**: Name and define some of what you deem as the main different parts of the ***socially just ethic***. Be sure to discuss how it is different from other ethical approaches.

**Answer:** According to the authors, socially just ethics (SJ ethics) are ways of making ethical decisions that focus on helping people who have been treated unfairly by society. The main parts include: 
  - Intersectionality - understanding that people can face multiple types of unfair treatment at the same time (like being discriminated against for both race and gender); 
  - Positionality - recognizing that where you come from in society affects your experiences; 
  - Privilege - acknowledging that some people have advantages others don't have; and 
  - Collective Action - working together in groups rather than alone to fix unfair systems.

  SJ ethics are different from other ethical approaches because traditional ethics focused on individual people trying to be "good," while SJ ethics focus on groups of people working together to change unfair systems. Instead of just trying to follow rules or do what's most efficient, SJ ethics specifically aim to help marginalized communities lead the way in fixing injustice and making society more fair for everyone.

### 3.2. Defining critical issues

**Question**: Briefly define each of the critical issues discussed: Cultural Imperialism, Marginalization, and Intersectional Oppression.

**Cultural Imperialism**: When powerful groups (like white colonizers) steal land, force people into slavery, and destroy other cultures by making everyone follow their ways. Today this happens when technology only works well for white English speakers and ignores other accents or languages.

**Marginalization**: When certain groups of people are pushed to the edges of society and not allowed to participate fully. Indigenous people have been marginalized through broken promises, stolen land, and policies that keep many of them poor.

**Intersectional Oppression**: When people face multiple types of unfair treatment at the same time. For example, Black mothers face both racism and sexism in healthcare, which creates unique problems that are different from facing just racism or just sexism alone.

### 3.3. Defining the 3Ps to help us recognize oppression

**Question**: Define each of the 3Ps below. Explain how each helps us recognize oppression.

**Power**: Power is when some groups have control over others through rules, systems, and deciding whose ideas matter. This helps us see oppression by showing who has control and who doesn't.

**Privilege**: Privilege means having advantages because of who you are (like being white or male). Some people can speak up about problems more easily than others. This helps us see oppression by showing who gets benefits in society.

**Positionality**: Positionality means understanding where you stand in society based on your identity (race, gender, class, etc.). It helps you see how your background shapes your experiences and viewpoint. This helps recognize oppression by making you aware of how different people experience the world differently based on who they are.

### 3.4. Why an SJ ethic?

**Question**: Why do Lindgren, Gerdes, and Lawrence position a "SJ ethic" as essential to the future of TPC? What types of problems does it help TPC tackle?

TPC has been supporting unfair systems without realizing it. Past ethics focused on individual "good behavior" but ignored bigger problems of inequality. SJ ethics focus on working together to fix unfair systems and help oppressed groups.

**Problems SJ ethics help solve:**

1. Language discrimination - Technologies like voice assistants only work well for white English speakers, excluding others.
2. Excluding marginalized groups - Some groups (like Indigenous people) are left out of important decisions.
3. Accent bias - Technologies that try to "fix" non-white accents instead of accepting them.
4. Data control - Big companies collect people's data without letting communities control their own information.
5. Multiple forms of unfairness - People face different types of discrimination at the same time

**Answer**: Graham et al say their 150,000+ pages of meeting transcripts is "big data" for technical communication. Even though it's not as huge as data in other fields like physics, it's still "big" because it's too much data for normal research methods to handle.

### 4.2. On SGA

**Question**: Summarize the following parts of SGA:

1. What TPC problems SGA addresses,
2. What data they used to demonstrate its potential,
3. What questions SGA data and methods enable TPC to ask, and
4. What methods it uses to do so.

**Answer**: SGA solves the problem that normal TPC research methods can't handle big data sets or tell us which writing techniques actually work best. Graham et al tested SGA using over 150,000 pages of FDA meeting transcripts to study drug company presentations, looking at who was in meetings, what evidence was used, and how people voted. This method lets TPC researchers ask new questions like: Do patient representatives change how meetings go? Do conflicts of interest affect decisions? Do companies change their presentations based on their audience? SGA answers these questions by combining writing studies with computer statistics, using tools like regression analysis and content analysis to find patterns in the data and figure out which presentation features are most likely to get approval.

## 5. "Science is a social endeavor."

**Question**: Discuss what this statement means to you. Then, discuss any connections, confusion, and/or questions that you recognize with a SJ ethic and structural perspective?

**Answer**: "Science is a social endeavor" means that scientific knowledge is created through people working together rather than individual scientists making discoveries alone. Scientists collaborate, share ideas, review each other's work, and build on previous research from the scientific community. The questions they choose to study, the methods they use, and how they interpret results are all influenced by social factors.

SJ ethics and structural perspective both say unfairness comes from how systems work, not just bad people doing bad things. Both want groups of people to work together to fix unfair systems. But there are problems; people who want social justice still have to work in unfair systems to make money and survive. And, again- when different types of unfairness happen at the same time, it's hard to know which one to fix first. So, should we focus on changing big systems or helping people who need help right now? And when we try to change power structures, might we accidentally create new unfair systems?
